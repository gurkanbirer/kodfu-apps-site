Delay Repay explained: how much you get for a late train in 2026

If your train in Great Britain gets you to your destination late, you can usually claim part or all of your fare back from the train company. This guide explains DR15 and DR30, the bands and amounts with worked examples, the 28-day deadline, season tickets and automatic claims.

What Delay Repay is

Delay Repay is the compensation scheme most train companies in Great Britain use when you arrive late. National Rail describes it as a nationwide scheme you can claim from if you are delayed for any reason on a participating service. You claim from the train company, on its own website or by post, not from National Rail or the website that sold you the ticket.

Delay Repay sits on top of a legal minimum. The National Rail Conditions of Travel (condition 33.3) guarantee compensation when you arrive 60 minutes or more late, regardless of fault: at least 50% of a single, or of the delayed portion of a return. Train companies may pay more than this, but not less, and Delay Repay is how most of them do it.

It adds up: GOV.UK figures show the train companies in the Department for Transport’s table paid about £150.6 million in passenger compensation in 2024 to 2025.

DR15, DR30 and own schemes

The name says when a company starts paying:

  • DR15: from 15 minutes late. Most companies, including Southern, Thameslink, Northern, Avanti West Coast, GWR, South Western Railway, Southeastern and TransPennine Express.
  • DR30: from 30 minutes late. CrossCountry, LNER, ScotRail, Caledonian Sleeper, Hull Trains and Lumo.
  • Own schemes: Grand Central (from 60 minutes), Heathrow Express (from 30 minutes, a share of the price) and Merseyrail (more than 30 minutes).

How much you get

The amount is a share of what you paid, rising with the delay at your destination. These are the DR15 bands as the train companies publish them:

Arrival delaySingleReturn, one way late
15 to 29 minutes25%12.5%
30 to 59 minutes50%25%
60 to 119 minutes100%50%
120 minutes or more100%100%

DR30 companies use the same table without the first row. Hull Trains and Lumo have no 120-minute band: from 60 minutes they pay the full single or half the return. On a return ticket the percentage is of the whole return fare, and a delay of 2 hours or more on either leg usually gets the whole return back.

You can never get back more than the price of your ticket (Conditions of Travel 32.2). For a season ticket the cap is one day’s travel.

How the delay is measured

  • At your destination. It is the delay arriving at the station where your journey ends, not the delay setting off (Conditions of Travel 32.1 and 33.1). A late first train that makes you miss a connection counts if you arrive late at the end: see missed connections.
  • Against the timetable in force that day. If an engineering-works or emergency timetable was published in advance, that is the baseline. A rail replacement bus is measured the same way.
  • For the train you planned to catch. The claim form asks for the train you intended to use, even if you ended up on a later one. Giving the train you actually caught is a common reason claims fail.
  • Whatever the cause, for an hour or more. A delay of 60 minutes or more is paid regardless of fault (32.1). For shorter delays a company may exclude causes entirely outside the railway’s control (33.4); its Passenger’s Charter lists them. You do not have to prove the delay was the company’s fault.

The 28-day deadline

The Conditions of Travel (33.1) say to claim within 28 days of completing the journey, unless the company gives longer. Companies word it differently: “within 28 days of travel”, “of the delay”, or that the claim must reach them within 28 days, which matters for postal claims. Claim within a few days if you can: you still have your ticket and remember the train.

What you need to claim

  • The journey: date, the stations you travelled from and to, the train you planned to catch and when you actually arrived.
  • Your ticket: a photo, scan or screenshot showing the ticket number, journey and price; or the eTicket reference or smartcard number. Several companies refuse receipts and booking emails that do not show a ticket number.
  • For Oyster or contactless pay as you go on National Rail, your TfL journey history.
  • How you want to be paid.

Only claim for journeys you actually made, with the real arrival time. Train companies check every claim against their own records.

Automatic and one-click claims

Many companies now raise a claim for you for some tickets bought direct from them, such as Advance tickets or smartcards you tap in and out with. Usually you still confirm it from an email. Examples: Avanti West Coast, GWR, South Western Railway, Northern, TransPennine Express, Chiltern, Lumo, Transport for Wales and Caledonian Sleeper (Automated or Automatic Delay Repay), LNER and West Midlands Railway (One-click), Southeastern (One Click), the GTR companies (Auto Delay Repay for Key Smartcards) and c2c (Automatic Delay Repay for its smartcard).

Do not claim twice. With some companies, a claim you make by hand cancels the automatic one. Check your email first. Tickets bought from other retailers, paper season tickets and many walk-up tickets still need a claim by hand.

How you are paid

Companies offer bank transfer, card, PayPal, cheque, rail vouchers or charity donations, in different mixes. The Conditions of Travel (34.1) require at least one money option, and payment within 14 days of the claim being agreed (33.2). Postal claims often have fewer options.

What is not covered

Delay Repay covers National Rail services in Great Britain. These have their own rules and are not covered by FareBack:

  • Transport for London services, including the Elizabeth line and London Overground, which have TfL’s own refund rules;
  • Eurostar;
  • NI Railways in Northern Ireland.

Refused claims, refunds and extra costs

  • Claim refused or underpaid? Ask the company to review it, then complain; after a final response or 40 working days the free Rail Ombudsman can decide. Step by step, with a letter to copy: challenge a rejected claim.
  • Train cancelled and you did not travel? That is a refund, not Delay Repay: cancelled trains.
  • Taxi, hotel or a missed flight? Delay Repay pays up to the ticket price. Other losses are at the company’s discretion (32.2) or a claim under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, which requires the service to be performed with reasonable care and skill. You cannot recover the same money twice. See GOV.UK, Consumer Rights Act: application to transport services. General information, not legal advice.

Delay Repay questions

How late does my train have to be to claim Delay Repay?

15 minutes with DR15 companies and 30 minutes with DR30 companies, measured at your destination. Every company must pay for 60 minutes or more.

How much Delay Repay will I get?

On DR15: 25% of a single for 15 to 29 minutes, 50% for 30 to 59 minutes and 100% from 60 minutes. On a return, half those shares of the return fare, and the whole return from 2 hours.

How long do I have to claim?

Usually 28 days from the journey. Some companies count from the delay or need the claim to arrive within 28 days, so claim early.

Can I claim if I bought my ticket on Trainline or another app?

Yes. You claim from the train company whose train delayed you, wherever you bought the ticket. Automatic claims usually only cover tickets bought direct from the train company, so with another retailer you claim by hand.

Can I claim Delay Repay on a season ticket?

Yes. Each journey is valued at the season price divided by a set number of journeys, such as 464 for an annual season, and the same percentages apply. See season tickets.
