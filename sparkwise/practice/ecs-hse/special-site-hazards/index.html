Asbestos, buried services, excavations, overhead lines, confined spaces and lone working.

  1. Question 1 · Choose one answer

    Which buildings may contain asbestos?

    1. Any built or refurbished before 2000
    2. Only buildings built before 1950
    3. Only industrial and commercial buildings
    4. Only buildings with flat roofs
    Show the answer

    Answer A: Any built or refurbished before 2000

    Asbestos was used widely until the full UK ban in 1999, so any building from before 2000 could contain it.

    Source: HSE: asbestos · Control of Asbestos Regs 2012

  2. Question 2 · Select TWO answers

    Which of these are common places to find asbestos in buildings from before 2000? Select TWO.

    1. Insulating board ceiling tiles
    2. Textured decorative ceiling coatings
    3. Modern PVC trunking and conduit
    4. Plasterboard installed in 2020
    Show the answer

    Answer A: Insulating board ceiling tiles, B: Textured decorative ceiling coatings

    Insulating board and textured coatings were common uses of asbestos. Modern plastics and recent plasterboard do not contain it.

    Source: HSE: asbestos · Control of Asbestos Regs 2012

  3. Question 3 · Choose one answer

    Who has the duty to manage asbestos in non-domestic premises?

    1. Every electrician who works there
    2. The local council's building control
    3. The company that made the asbestos
    4. The person in charge of maintenance
    Show the answer

    Answer D: The person in charge of maintenance

    The duty holder, usually the owner or whoever is responsible for maintenance and repair, must find, record and manage asbestos.

    Source: Control of Asbestos Regulations 2012 regs 4, 10

  4. Question 4 · Choose one answer

    Before digging near where buried cables may be, what should you do?

    1. Get service plans and use a cable avoidance tool
    2. Dig with a pick to find them quickly
    3. Assume there are no services if none are on the plan
    4. Ask the client whether they remember any
    Show the answer

    Answer A: Get service plans and use a cable avoidance tool

    Plans help you plan, and a locator used by a trained person helps find what is actually there.

    Source: HSE: Avoiding danger from underground services (HSG47)

  5. Question 5 · Choose one answer

    How should you dig close to a located buried cable?

    1. With a pick or fork to loosen the ground
    2. With a mechanical excavator to finish quickly
    3. By hand with a spade or shovel, in trial holes
    4. By driving a steel spike down to find it
    Show the answer

    Answer C: By hand with a spade or shovel, in trial holes

    Hand digging with spades and shovels is less likely to damage the cable than picks, forks or machines.

    Source: HSE: Avoiding danger from underground services (HSG47)

  6. Question 6 · Choose one answer

    When must an excavation be inspected by a competent person?

    1. Only once, when it is first dug
    2. Once a month by the site manager
    3. At the start of every shift
    4. Only after someone has been injured
    Show the answer

    Answer C: At the start of every shift

    A competent person inspects at the start of every shift and after any event that may have affected safety, such as a fall of material.

    Source: CDM 2015 reg 22 (excavations)

  7. Question 7 · Choose one answer

    Unless the line owner confirms otherwise, how should overhead power lines be treated?

    1. As dead
    2. As phone lines
    3. As disused
    4. As live
    Show the answer

    Answer D: As live

    Overhead lines can carry very high voltages. Assume they are live until the owner confirms that they are dead.

    Source: HSE: Avoiding danger from overhead lines (GS6)

  8. Question 8 · Choose one answer

    Why should ladders and scaffold tubes be carried horizontally near overhead lines?

    1. Because they are lighter that way
    2. To keep them well below the wires
    3. So they do not block the footpath
    4. So the wind cannot catch them
    Show the answer

    Answer B: To keep them well below the wires

    Long conductive objects carried upright can reach or approach the wires.

    Source: HSE: Avoiding danger from overhead lines (GS6)

  9. Question 9 · Choose one answer

    What must be in place before anyone enters a confined space?

    1. A warning sign fixed at the entrance only
    2. A torch and a working mobile phone
    3. A colleague waiting in the canteen
    4. A safe system and rescue arrangements
    Show the answer

    Answer D: A safe system and rescue arrangements

    Entry needs a safe system, often a permit and gas testing, and rescue arrangements ready before anyone goes in.

    Source: Confined Spaces Regulations 1997

  10. Question 10 · Choose one answer

    At a petrol station forecourt, what is special about electrical equipment installed in the hazardous zones?

    1. It can be standard domestic-grade equipment
    2. It must run at 110 V for safety
    3. It must be rated for explosive atmospheres
    4. It must be mounted at least 2 m high
    Show the answer

    Answer C: It must be rated for explosive atmospheres

    Ordinary equipment can spark and ignite flammable vapour. Equipment in these zones must be designed and certified for that use.

    Source: DSEAR 2002 reg 7 · BS EN 60079 (Ex equipment)

Revision notes

Asbestos: where it is and why it matters

Asbestos was widely used in buildings until it was fully banned in the UK in 1999, so any building built or refurbished before 2000 may contain it. Electricians meet it in insulating board ceiling tiles and panels, old fuse board backing boards and flash guards, textured ceiling coatings, floor tiles, pipe lagging and cement sheets. Breathing in the fibres can cause mesothelioma, asbestos-related lung cancer, asbestosis and pleural thickening. These diseases appear decades later and are usually incurable. All types of asbestos are dangerous, whatever their colour.

Asbestos: before and during work

Whoever is responsible for maintaining non-domestic premises has a duty to manage asbestos: find it, record it in an asbestos register and plan, and share that information with anyone who could disturb it. Ask to see it before drilling, cutting or lifting anything in an older building. Intrusive refurbishment needs a refurbishment and demolition survey first. Anyone liable to disturb asbestos needs asbestos awareness training. If you find suspect material, stop work at once, do not disturb it further, keep others away and report it.

Buried services

Underground cables and pipes are hard to see and easy to hit. HSE guidance (HSG47) sets out three steps: plan the work using service records, but remember plans are not exact and many services are not shown; locate services with a cable avoidance tool (CAT) and signal generator used by a trained person, and mark their positions on the ground; then dig safely, hand-digging trial holes near located services with spades and shovels rather than picks or forks. Treat every buried cable as live until proved otherwise.

Excavations

Trench sides can collapse without warning, and a cubic metre of soil weighs over a tonne. Even shallow excavations can bury someone, so support the sides, batter them back or step them where there is a risk. Protect edges with barriers, keep spoil, materials and plant away from the edge, and use stop blocks so vehicles cannot tip in. Provide a safe way in and out, such as a secured ladder. A competent person must inspect excavations at the start of each shift and after any event that may have affected their safety.

Overhead power lines

Treat all overhead lines as live unless the line owner confirms otherwise. Electricity can arc across a gap, so you do not have to touch a line to be killed. Where possible, plan work away from lines or ask the network operator to divert or switch them off. Otherwise use barriers, goalposts and height limits to keep plant, scaffold tubes, ladders and long materials well clear. If a machine touches a line, the operator should stay in the cab unless there is fire; if they must leave, they jump clear without touching the machine and the ground together.

Confined spaces

A confined space is a substantially enclosed place where a serious injury could result from hazards such as lack of oxygen, toxic gas or fumes, flooding, fire or explosion, or extreme heat. Electricians may need to enter cable chambers, ducts, tanks, voids and plant rooms that fit this description. Under the Confined Spaces Regulations 1997, avoid entry if the work can be done from outside. Otherwise follow a safe system of work, often with a permit, gas testing and trained people, and have emergency rescue arrangements in place before anyone goes in.

Lone working and other special hazards

Working alone is not illegal, but it must be risk assessed. Lone workers need a way to raise the alarm, a check-in system and a supervisor who knows where they are. Some work, such as confined space entry or justified live working, should never be done alone. Other special hazards include explosive atmospheres at places such as petrol stations, where only specially protected equipment may be used, contaminated land, old lead paint, and working in occupied buildings where the public must be kept away from the work.
