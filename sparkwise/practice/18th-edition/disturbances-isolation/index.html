Voltage disturbances, isolation and switching: free 18th Edition practice questions

  1. Question 1 · Choose one answer

    What are the two main origins of transient overvoltages considered by Section 443?

    1. Atmospheric (lightning) and switching
    2. Harmonics and excessive voltage drop
    3. Earth leakage and circuit overload
    4. Static build-up and induction heating
    Show the answer

    Answer A: Atmospheric (lightning) and switching

    Section 443 deals with transients from lightning and from switching operations in the network or installation.

    Where to look: Reg 443.1

  2. Question 2 · Choose one answer

    Which overvoltage category applies to equipment at the origin, such as the electricity meter?

    1. Category I
    2. Category II
    3. Category III
    4. Category IV
    Show the answer

    Answer D: Category IV

    Category IV equipment at or near the origin must withstand the highest impulse, 6 kV for a 230/400 V system.

    Where to look: Table 443.2

  3. Question 3 · Choose one answer

    A distribution board and its fixed socket-outlets belong to which overvoltage category?

    1. Category I
    2. Category II
    3. Category III
    4. Category IV
    Show the answer

    Answer C: Category III

    Fixed installation equipment such as distribution boards, switches and socket-outlets is category III, withstanding 4 kV.

    Where to look: Table 443.2

  4. Question 4 · Choose one answer

    Where none of the listed serious consequences apply, what does Regulation 443.4.1 require?

    1. No surge protection is needed at all
    2. SPDs required unless the owner declines
    3. A lightning protection system must be fitted
    4. An AFDD must be fitted to every circuit
    Show the answer

    Answer B: SPDs required unless the owner declines

    In all other cases SPDs must still be fitted unless the owner of the installation declares they are not needed, accepting any loss or damage as tolerable. The old exemption for low-value single dwellings was removed in Amendment 2.

    Where to look: Reg 443.4.1

  5. Question 5 · Choose one answer

    Which practice helps limit electromagnetic disturbance in an installation?

    1. Bundling power and data cables together
    2. Running cables to form large inductive loops
    3. Separating power and signal cables, no big loops
    4. Removing bonding from metal cable trays
    Show the answer

    Answer C: Separating power and signal cables, no big loops

    Keeping power and signal cables apart and avoiding inductive loops reduces induced interference.

    Where to look: Reg 444.4

  6. Question 6 · Choose one answer

    Which of these must NOT be used as an isolating device?

    1. A linked switch with visible contact indication
    2. A semiconductor device
    3. A circuit-breaker suitable for isolation
    4. A fuse carrier that can be removed
    Show the answer

    Answer B: A semiconductor device

    Semiconductor devices do not provide a reliable physical gap, so they must not be used for isolation.

    Where to look: Reg 537.2

  7. Question 7 · True or false?

    A device used for isolation must clearly and reliably show when its contacts are open.

    1. True
    2. False
    Show the answer

    Answer A: True

    The open position must be clearly indicated so the person relying on it knows the circuit is isolated.

    Where to look: Reg 537.2

  8. Question 8 · Choose one answer

    What is the purpose of emergency switching?

    1. To switch lighting on and off normally
    2. To remove an unexpected danger quickly
    3. To allow safe testing of circuits
    4. To reduce energy use out of hours
    Show the answer

    Answer B: To remove an unexpected danger quickly

    Emergency switching quickly removes an unexpected danger, ideally with a single action acting directly on the supply.

    Where to look: Reg 465.1

  9. Question 9 · Choose one answer

    Which chapter of Part 4 covers isolation and switching?

    1. Chapter 41
    2. Chapter 43
    3. Chapter 44
    4. Chapter 46
    Show the answer

    Answer D: Chapter 46

    Chapter 46 covers isolation and switching. Chapter 53 covers the selection and erection of the devices themselves.

    Where to look: Chapter 46

  10. Question 10 · Choose one answer

    Where in BS 7671 would you look for the rated impulse withstand voltages for each overvoltage category?

    1. Table 41.1
    2. Table 443.2
    3. Table 52.3
    4. Table 64
    Show the answer

    Answer B: Table 443.2

    Table 443.2 lists the required impulse withstand voltages for categories I to IV.

    Where to look: Table 443.2

Revision notes

Overvoltage categories

Transient overvoltages come from lightning (atmospheric origin) and from switching. Chapter 44 groups equipment by the impulse voltage it must withstand. For a 230/400 V system, category IV equipment at or near the origin, such as meters and the main protective device, withstands 6 kV; category III fixed installation equipment, such as distribution boards, switches and socket-outlets, 4 kV; category II equipment such as household appliances and portable tools, 2.5 kV; and category I sensitive equipment, 1.5 kV. Surge protective devices keep transients below these levels.

When surge protection is required

Section 443 requires protection against transient overvoltages where the consequences could cause serious injury or loss of life, interrupt public services or damage cultural heritage, interrupt commercial or industrial activity, or affect a large number of co-located people. In all other cases SPDs must still be fitted unless the owner of the installation declares them unnecessary and accepts the risk. Amendment 2 deleted the old calculated risk level (CRL) method and the low-value dwelling exception.

Other voltage disturbances

Section 442 deals with temporary overvoltages caused by earth faults in the high-voltage system or faults in the low-voltage supply. Section 444 covers electromagnetic disturbances: avoid large inductive loops, separate power and data cabling, provide bonding networks and prefer a TN-S arrangement downstream of the origin where there is a lot of IT equipment. Section 445 covers undervoltage: where a voltage drop and later restoration could cause danger, for example a motor restarting unexpectedly, suitable precautions such as no-volt release starters are needed.

Isolation

Isolation cuts off the supply from every source so that work can be done safely. Every circuit must be capable of being isolated from each live supply conductor, though in TN systems the neutral need not be isolated where it is reliably at earth potential. In TT and IT systems all live conductors, including the neutral, are isolated. Isolating devices must show clearly when they are open, be secured against unintentional closing, for example by locking off, and must not be semiconductor devices. In a single-phase household installation the main switch breaks both line and neutral.

Switching off for mechanical maintenance

This switching protects people doing non-electrical work, such as cleaning, replacing parts or changing lamps, where the equipment could cause burns or injury from mechanical movement. The device must be suitably placed, readily identifiable, and arranged so that it cannot be switched back on by accident, for example by locking it off or keeping it under the worker's control. It is different from isolation, which protects people working on live parts of the electrical installation.

Emergency and functional switching

Emergency switching removes an unexpected danger quickly, ideally with a single action acting directly on the supply. Emergency switches and stop buttons should be readily accessible where danger could arise and are usually red on a contrasting background. Functional switching controls equipment in normal operation, such as a light switch or a thermostat; it does not have to break all live conductors. Firefighter's switches for high-voltage signs and certain outdoor installations are a special case covered in Chapter 53.
