Delay Repay explained: train delay compensation in Great Britain

If your train in Great Britain arrives late, you can often claim part of your fare back, whatever caused the delay. This guide explains how Delay Repay works, how much you might get, how long you have to claim and what to have ready.

What Delay Repay is

Delay Repay is the compensation scheme most train companies in Great Britain use when you arrive late. It is "no fault": you can claim whatever caused the delay, whether it was the weather, a signal failure or something else. The delay is measured at the station on your ticket where you finish your journey, against the normal timetable or a timetable published in advance, for example for weekend engineering works.

You claim from the train company, not from National Rail, and most claims start on the train company's own website. Some companies also take postal claims, with a form from their website or a staffed station. National Rail's find a train company page links to each one.

It adds up: GOV.UK figures show the train companies in the Department for Transport's table paid about £150.6 million in passenger compensation in 2024 to 2025.

DR15 and DR30

These names say when a train company's scheme starts to pay:

  • DR15: you can claim when you arrive at least 15 minutes late.
  • DR30: you can claim when you arrive at least 30 minutes late.

Most companies use DR15. Of the 15 train companies in GOV.UK's compensation table, all pay for delays of 30 minutes or more and 13 also pay from 15 minutes. Each company sets its threshold in its Passenger's Charter, so check before you rely on it.

How much you can get

The amount is a share of what you paid, and it goes up with the delay. These are the commonly published Delay Repay bands; each train company sets its own rules, and a company may pay more.

Compensation is never more than the price of the ticket. A few companies run their own schemes with different bands, so the train company always makes the final decision on whether to pay and how much.

How long you have to claim

Usually 28 days from the date you travelled. Train companies word this differently, and some count from the day of the delay or need a postal claim to arrive within the period, so check the company's page. Claiming soon after the journey is easiest: you still have your ticket and remember the times.

What you need to claim

  • The journey details: date, the stations you travelled from and to, the train you caught and when you actually arrived.
  • Your ticket: a photo, scan or screenshot of it, or the booking reference or smartcard number for an e-ticket or smartcard.
  • What you paid, if the form asks.
  • How you want to be paid. Companies offer different methods, such as bank transfer, card, PayPal, cheque or rail vouchers.

Only claim for journeys you actually made, and give the real arrival time. Train companies check claims against their own records.

Some companies pay automatically for some tickets, for example certain smartcards when you touch in and out, or Advance tickets bought direct. If yours does, don't claim the same journey twice: a manual claim can replace the automatic one.

Season tickets

You can claim on a season ticket too. The train company works out a value for each journey by dividing the season price by a set number of journeys, then applies the usual percentages to that value. The number depends on the company and the ticket length. Thameslink, for example, uses 464 journeys for an annual season, 40 for a monthly and 10 for a weekly. Some companies use different rules again, so check your company's page or use a calculator that knows its rules.

Because each delay on a season ticket is worth a small amount, commuters often miss claims. Over a year they can add up.

Cancelled trains and refunds

If your train is cancelled and you take a later one, the delay is usually measured from the time you should have arrived to the time you actually arrived, and you claim Delay Repay in the normal way.

If you decide not to travel at all because of the disruption, that is a refund, not compensation: ask the retailer you bought the ticket from. National Rail says it should be refunded without a fee. See National Rail's compensation and refunds page.

What is not covered

Delay Repay covers National Rail services in Great Britain. These have their own rules and are not covered by FareBack:

  • Transport for London (TfL) services, which have their own delay refund schemes;
  • Eurostar;
  • NI Railways in Northern Ireland.

If your claim is refused

  1. Complain to the train company with your journey details. ORR says you should usually get a full reply within 20 working days.
  2. Ask it to look again if you are not happy with the answer.
  3. Go to the Rail Ombudsman. It is free and independent, and its decisions are binding on the train company. See the Rail Ombudsman and ORR's know your rail rights.

The Consumer Rights Act

Since 01/10/2016 the Consumer Rights Act 2015 has applied in full to mainline passenger rail services. The government said that from that date passengers can seek redress under the Act if a train company does not provide its service with reasonable care and skill, and that the industry schemes such as Delay Repay stay the main way to claim. See GOV.UK, Consumer Rights Act: application to transport services. This is general information, not legal advice.
