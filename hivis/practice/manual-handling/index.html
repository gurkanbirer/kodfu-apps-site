Manual handling: free HS&E test practice questions

Avoiding, assessing and reducing lifting risks, safe technique and handling aids.

6 original questions from the HiVis app for the CITB HS&E Operatives test, each with the answer and an explanation. No sign-up, nothing saved. These are not the real test questions: no app or website can show you those.

  1. Question 1 · Choose one answer

    Under the Manual Handling Operations Regulations, what should an employer do first?

    1. Avoid hazardous manual handling if reasonably practicable
    2. Train workers so they can lift heavier loads safely
    3. Issue every worker with a back support belt to wear
    4. Pay extra to the people who do heavy lifting work
    Show the answer

    Answer A: Avoid hazardous manual handling if reasonably practicable

    The first step is to avoid hazardous manual handling, for example by using mechanical aids or delivering materials where they are needed.

    Source: Manual Handling Operations Regulations 1992 reg 4(1)(a)

  2. Question 2 · Choose one answer

    When lifting a load, where should you hold it?

    1. At arm's length to keep it away from your body
    2. Above your shoulders
    3. Close to your waist
    4. Out to one side
    Show the answer

    Answer C: Close to your waist

    Keeping the load close to your waist reduces the strain on your back. Holding it away from your body multiplies the force on your spine.

    Source: HSE INDG143

  3. Question 3 · Choose one answer

    You need to move a load that is clearly too heavy and awkward for you to lift on your own.

    What should you do?

    1. Try it anyway and stop if it hurts
    2. Drag it along the floor
    3. Ask for help or a handling aid
    4. Lift it in quick jerks
    Show the answer

    Answer C: Ask for help or a handling aid

    If a load is too heavy or awkward, get help or use a handling aid. Trying it alone risks a serious injury.

    Source: Manual Handling Operations Regulations 1992 reg 4 and reg 5

  4. Question 4 · Choose one answer

    If you must move a heavy trolley, which is usually better?

    1. Pushing, as you can use your weight and see ahead
    2. Pulling, as it always feels lighter to move that way
    3. Dragging it behind you using only one hand
    4. Moving it sideways so you can see both ends
    Show the answer

    Answer A: Pushing, as you can use your weight and see ahead

    Pushing usually lets you use your body weight and see where you are going. Pulling can strain your back and shoulders.

    Source: HSE INDG478 pushing and pulling guidance

  5. Question 5 · Choose one answer

    Is there a legal maximum weight that one worker may lift at work?

    1. No, the risk must be assessed for each task
    2. Yes, 25 kg for every worker on every task
    3. Yes, 50 kg if the worker has been trained
    4. No, workers may lift anything they feel able to
    Show the answer

    Answer A: No, the risk must be assessed for each task

    The law sets no weight limit because the risk depends on more than weight: posture, distance, how often, the person and the conditions. HSE guideline weights only flag tasks for a closer look; they are not safe limits.

    Source: HSE: Manual handling risk filters

  6. Question 6 · Choose one answer

    Where is the best place to store heavy items that are handled often?

    1. Around waist height
    2. On the floor at the back
    3. On the highest shelf
    4. Under the workbench
    Show the answer

    Answer A: Around waist height

    Lifting from the floor or above the shoulders puts much more strain on the back and shoulders. Storing heavy items at about waist height cuts bending and reaching every time they are handled.

    Source: HSE INDG143

Revision notes

Why it matters

Lifting, carrying, pushing and pulling cause a large share of work-related injuries and ill health in construction, mostly to backs, shoulders, knees and wrists. Damage often builds up slowly from repeated handling, but a single bad lift can also cause lasting harm. Musculoskeletal disorders can end careers and affect everyday life, so handling deserves the same planning as any other hazard.

The law

The Manual Handling Operations Regulations 1992 set out an order for employers. First, avoid hazardous manual handling so far as is reasonably practicable, for example by using machinery. Second, assess any handling that cannot be avoided. Third, reduce the risk as far as reasonably practicable, and give information about the weight of loads. Workers must make full and proper use of the systems of work and equipment provided.

Assessing the risk: TILE

A simple way to assess a handling task is TILE. Task: does it involve twisting, stooping, reaching, long distances or frequent repetition? Individual: is the person fit, trained and able to do it? Load: how heavy, bulky, unstable, sharp, hot or hard to grip is it? Environment: is the space cramped, the floor uneven or slippery, are there stairs, poor lighting or wind?

Reducing the risk

Use handling aids wherever possible: sack trucks, pallet trucks, hoists, block grabs and vacuum lifters for kerbs and slabs. Order materials in smaller units, such as 25 kg cement bags, and get them delivered close to where they will be used. Store heavy items at about waist height. When a team lift is needed, plan it and agree one person to give the commands.

Good handling technique

Plan the lift: know where the load is going, clear the route and decide whether you need help. Stand with your feet apart for a stable base, bend your knees, and keep your back in its natural curve. Get a firm grip, keep the load close to your waist and lift smoothly without jerking. Move your feet to turn rather than twisting. Put the load down, then adjust it. Pushing is usually better than pulling.

Weight guidelines are not limits

The law does not set a maximum weight a person may lift. HSE publishes guideline weights to help spot handling that needs a closer look, but they are not safe limits. The risk depends on the task, the person, the load and the surroundings: a load that is fine held at waist height close to the body can be risky lifted from the floor, at arm's length, above the shoulders or many times an hour. More than about one lift every two minutes calls for a more detailed assessment.
