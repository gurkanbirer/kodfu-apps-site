Legal duties and site rules: free HS&E test practice questions

Who is responsible for what on site, the main health and safety laws, inductions, RAMS and what inspectors can do.

6 original questions from the HiVis app for the CITB HS&E Operatives test, each with the answer and an explanation. No sign-up, nothing saved. These are not the real test questions: no app or website can show you those.

  1. Question 1 · Choose one answer

    What is the main law covering health and safety at work in Great Britain?

    1. The Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974
    2. The Construction Products Regulations 2013 (as amended)
    3. The Building Regulations 2010
    4. The Employment Rights Act 1996
    Show the answer

    Answer A: The Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974

    It sets the general duties of employers, workers and others, and most other safety rules, such as CDM, COSHH and the Work at Height Regulations, are made under it. That is why its duties apply on every site.

    Source: Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974

  2. Question 2 · Choose one answer

    What is a method statement?

    1. A document setting out how a job will be done safely
    2. A list of all the materials needed for a job
    3. A record of the hours each worker has done
    4. A contract between the client and the contractor
    Show the answer

    Answer A: A document setting out how a job will be done safely

    A method statement describes the sequence of work and the controls needed to do it safely. It is often paired with the risk assessment as RAMS.

    Source: Management Regulations 1999 reg 3

  3. Question 3 · Choose one answer

    What is a toolbox talk?

    1. A training course on how to use power tools safely
    2. A short briefing on a particular hazard or task
    3. A meeting to plan which tools to buy next
    4. A formal meeting to deal with discipline
    Show the answer

    Answer B: A short briefing on a particular hazard or task

    A toolbox talk refreshes a specific hazard or control just before the work, when it matters most. Short, practical briefings at the workplace help everyone in the team follow the same safe method.

    Source: HSE and industry guidance on toolbox talks and briefings

  4. Question 4 · Choose one answer

    Why do workers under 18 need extra protection on site?

    1. They are not allowed to work in construction
    2. They must always work alone to learn quickly
    3. They may lack the experience to recognise risks
    4. They are not covered by health and safety law
    Show the answer

    Answer C: They may lack the experience to recognise risks

    Young people may be unaware of risks and keen to impress. Employers must assess the risks to them specifically and supervise them properly.

    Source: Management Regulations 1999 reg 19

  5. Question 5 · Choose one answer

    Who should explain the RAMS for a task to you before you start?

    1. Nobody, as you can read them in the site office
    2. Your supervisor, in a briefing before the work
    3. The client's architect, at the design meeting
    4. The supplier, when the materials are delivered
    Show the answer

    Answer B: Your supervisor, in a briefing before the work

    Your supervisor or the person in charge should brief you on the RAMS before you start, so you understand the method and controls.

    Source: Management Regulations 1999 regs 10 and 13

  6. Question 6 · Choose one answer

    You are working under a permit to work. The permit runs out at 12:00 and the job will not be finished by then.

    What should you do?

    1. Stop by 12:00 and ask for it to be renewed
    2. Carry on, as the job is nearly finished
    3. Change the time on the permit yourself
    4. Ask a workmate to sign a new permit for you
    Show the answer

    Answer A: Stop by 12:00 and ask for it to be renewed

    A permit is only valid for the time and conditions written on it. Once it expires, nobody has checked that the precautions are still in place, so stop and let the authorised person review and reissue it.

    Source: HSE guidance on permit-to-work systems

Revision notes

The main law

The Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 is the main health and safety law in Great Britain. Employers must, so far as is reasonably practicable, protect their employees: safe equipment and ways of working, information, training and supervision, a safe workplace and proper welfare. They must also protect other people affected by their work, such as the public. Employers with five or more employees must have a written health and safety policy.

Your duties as a worker

Workers have legal duties too. You must take reasonable care of your own health and safety and that of anyone affected by what you do or fail to do, and co-operate with your employer on safety. Nobody may interfere with or misuse anything provided for health and safety, such as guardrails, signs or fire extinguishers. You must use equipment as trained and report dangers and shortcomings in safety arrangements.

Risk assessments and method statements

Employers must assess the risks of their work and decide how to control them. On site this is usually set out in risk assessments and method statements (RAMS), which describe how a job will be done safely, step by step. You should be briefed on the RAMS before you start and follow them. If the job changes or the method does not work on the day, stop and ask so it can be reviewed.

CDM and how the site is run

The Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015 (CDM) set out who does what on a construction project. On sites with more than one contractor, the principal contractor plans and coordinates the work, sets the site rules, provides site inductions and makes sure welfare facilities are in place. Each contractor plans and supervises its own work. Workers must co-operate, follow the site rules and report anything likely to put people in danger.

Inductions, briefings and toolbox talks

Before you start on a site you should get an induction covering the site rules, main hazards, emergency arrangements, welfare and who to report to. Toolbox talks are short briefings on a particular hazard or task. You may be asked to sign to say you attended and understood. If anything is unclear, or not in a language you understand well, say so and ask for it to be explained before you sign.

Inspectors and enforcement

Health and Safety Executive (HSE) inspectors, or local authority inspectors for some workplaces, can enter a workplace at any reasonable time, look around, take photographs and samples and interview people. They can issue an improvement notice, giving time to put something right, or a prohibition notice, which stops a dangerous activity, often immediately. They can also prosecute. You must co-operate with an inspector and answer questions honestly.

Competence, consultation and young workers

Being competent means having the right skills, knowledge, training and experience for the job. A card shows your training or qualifications but does not replace good supervision. Employers must consult workers about health and safety, directly or through safety representatives, and display the "Health and safety law" poster or give out the leaflet. Workers under 18 need a specific risk assessment and closer supervision because of their inexperience.

Stopping unsafe work

If you believe there is serious and imminent danger, stop work, make it safe if you can, get clear and report it. If you are asked to do something unsafe, explain why and raise it with your supervisor or site manager. The law protects workers from being punished for raising genuine safety concerns or leaving a place of serious and imminent danger.

Notifying HSE about a project

Under CDM 2015 the client must notify HSE, on form F10, before construction starts if the work is expected to last longer than 30 working days with more than 20 workers on site at the same time at any point, or to exceed 500 person days. A copy of the notice is displayed on site so workers can read it. Notification does not change anyone's duties: CDM applies to every construction project, whatever its size.
