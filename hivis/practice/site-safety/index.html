Site housekeeping and access: free HS&E test practice questions

Keeping site tidy, safe access routes, lighting, storage of materials and protecting the public.

6 original questions from the HiVis app for the CITB HS&E Operatives test, each with the answer and an explanation. No sign-up, nothing saved. These are not the real test questions: no app or website can show you those.

  1. Question 1 · Choose one answer

    What does "tidy as you go" mean on site?

    1. Tidy your area once a week
    2. Leave tidying to the site cleaner
    3. Clear waste and offcuts as you work
    4. Tidy up only when the client visits
    Show the answer

    Answer C: Clear waste and offcuts as you work

    Clearing waste as you work keeps walkways and work areas free of trip hazards and reduces fire risk throughout the day.

    Source: CDM 2015 reg 18 (good order and site security)

  2. Question 2 · Choose one answer

    A barrier has been put across a shortcut you normally use to reach your work area. What should you do?

    1. Climb over it, as you know the route well
    2. Move it aside and put it back afterwards
    3. Use the marked walkway, even if it takes longer
    4. Use the route only when nobody is watching
    Show the answer

    Answer C: Use the marked walkway, even if it takes longer

    Barriers are put up for a reason, such as an opening, overhead work or moving plant. Use the marked route.

    Source: CDM 2015 reg 17

  3. Question 3 · Choose one answer

    How should a stack of pipes or tubes be stored?

    1. Stood upright and leant against a wall
    2. Piled as high as possible to save space
    3. On level ground with chocks to stop them rolling
    4. Leaning against the site hoarding or fence
    Show the answer

    Answer C: On level ground with chocks to stop them rolling

    Round items roll easily. Store them on firm, level ground with chocks or wedges and keep stacks to a stable height.

    Source: CDM 2015 reg 18

  4. Question 4 · Choose one answer

    You are the last person leaving your area at the end of the day. A ladder is still leaning against the scaffold, giving easy access to the first lift.

    What should you do?

    1. Leave it, as the site gates will be locked tonight
    2. Remove it or fit the ladder guard, then tell your supervisor
    3. Lay it flat across the scaffold platform for the night
    4. Leave it but put a warning sign at the bottom of it
    Show the answer

    Answer B: Remove it or fit the ladder guard, then tell your supervisor

    Children and trespassers may get onto a site out of hours. Removing or guarding ladders stops anyone climbing the scaffold and falling.

    Source: CDM 2015 reg 18

  5. Question 5 · Choose one answer

    You are working at ground level. Above you, another trade is working on a floor edge with no toe boards, and offcuts keep dropping near you.

    What should you do?

    1. Carry on, as you are already wearing a safety helmet
    2. Throw the offcuts back up to them
    3. Move out of the area and tell your supervisor
    4. Shout at them and carry on working below
    Show the answer

    Answer C: Move out of the area and tell your supervisor

    Falling objects can kill even through a helmet. Get clear of the drop zone and report it so the edge is properly protected.

    Source: Work at Height Regulations 2005 reg 10

  6. Question 6 · Choose one answer

    Barriers and signs mark an exclusion zone below roofers working overhead. The quickest way to the stores is through it.

    What should you do?

    1. Go the long way round the zone
    2. Walk through quickly, wearing a helmet
    3. Move the barrier and put it back after
    4. Run through when the roofers look busy
    Show the answer

    Answer A: Go the long way round the zone

    An exclusion zone keeps people out of the area where tools or materials might fall. A helmet will not stop a heavy object. Keep to routes outside the zone, even if it takes longer.

    Source: Work at Height Regulations 2005 reg 11

Revision notes

Housekeeping

Slips and trips are one of the most common causes of injury on site, and most are caused by poor housekeeping. Tidy as you go: clear offcuts, packaging and waste into the skips as you work instead of leaving it all to the end of the day. Clean up spills straight away or cover them and warn others. Keep walkways, stairs and work areas clear of materials, tools and trailing cables and hoses.

Access routes

Sites set out safe routes for people to reach their work. Use them, even when a shortcut looks quicker. Never climb over barriers, walk under loads, cut through plant areas or climb on stacked materials to get somewhere. If your route is blocked or unsafe, report it and wait for it to be made safe rather than finding your own way around.

Lighting

Good lighting lets you see hazards. Work areas, stairs, walkways and access routes must be properly lit, especially in winter, inside buildings and below ground. If lights fail or an area is too dark to work safely, stop and tell your supervisor. Temporary lighting should be fixed where it cannot be knocked or trip people, and emergency lighting may be needed on escape routes.

Storing materials

Stack materials on firm, level ground, not too high, and in a way that stops them sliding, rolling or toppling. Chock pipes and tubes, keep heavy items low, and leave space to get to them safely. Do not stack materials against hoardings, temporary fences or cabin walls, near open edges, or where they block walkways, fire points or emergency exits.

Signing in and keeping the public out

Sign in when you arrive and out when you leave, so everyone can be accounted for in an emergency. Visitors should be escorted. Sites must be secured against unauthorised entry, especially children, who may see a site as a playground. At the end of the day, lock plant and remove keys, take down or guard ladders, cover or fence openings and close gates.

Lone working and other trades

Working alone is only allowed when it has been planned, with a way to raise the alarm and regular contact with someone. High-risk jobs, such as work in confined spaces or near exposed live conductors, usually need more than one person. Be aware of other trades working above, below or next to you, keep out of their exclusion zones, and do not use or move their equipment without asking.
