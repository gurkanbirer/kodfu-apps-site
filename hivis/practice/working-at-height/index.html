Working at height: free HS&E test practice questions

The hierarchy for work at height, ladders, stepladders, towers, scaffolds, MEWPs, roofs and openings.

6 original questions from the HiVis app for the CITB HS&E Operatives test, each with the answer and an explanation. No sign-up, nothing saved. These are not the real test questions: no app or website can show you those.

  1. Question 1 · Choose one answer

    Under the Work at Height Regulations, what should be considered first?

    1. Whether the work can be done without working at height
    2. Which type of harness each person should wear
    3. How many ladders will be needed for the job
    4. Whether a safety net can be hired in time
    Show the answer

    Answer A: Whether the work can be done without working at height

    The first step is to avoid work at height where reasonably practicable, for example by assembling items at ground level and lifting them into place.

    Source: Work at Height Regulations 2005 reg 6

  2. Question 2 · Choose one answer

    You have been asked to paint a high ceiling, which will take most of the day. The only access equipment on the floor is an extension ladder.

    What should you do?

    1. Use the ladder and take regular breaks
    2. Tie two ladders together so you can reach more easily
    3. Ask your supervisor for a tower or podium instead
    4. Use the ladder but keep one foot on the wall
    Show the answer

    Answer C: Ask your supervisor for a tower or podium instead

    Ladders are only for short, light work. A long job like this needs a safer working platform, such as a mobile tower or podium steps.

    Source: Work at Height Regulations 2005 reg 7 and Sch 6

  3. Question 3 · Choose one answer

    Before you climb a mobile tower, what should you check?

    1. That it has been moved since yesterday
    2. That it reaches the full height of the building being worked on
    3. That the wheels are braked and stabilisers are fitted
    4. That the platform has a ladder on top of it
    Show the answer

    Answer C: That the wheels are braked and stabilisers are fitted

    Unbraked wheels or missing stabilisers can let the tower move or overturn. Check both, along with guardrails and toe boards, before you climb.

    Source: PASMA and HSE guidance on mobile access towers

  4. Question 4 · Choose one answer

    What is the minimum height of the main guardrail on a working platform?

    1. 600 mm
    2. 800 mm
    3. 950 mm
    4. 1,500 mm
    Show the answer

    Answer C: 950 mm

    A main guardrail at least 950 mm high stops a person toppling over, and an intermediate rail, leaving no gap over 470 mm, stops them slipping under. Toe boards stop tools and materials falling.

    Source: Work at Height Regulations 2005 Sch 2

  5. Question 5 · Choose one answer

    Why must there be a rescue plan whenever a fall arrest harness is used?

    1. So the employer can make a claim on its insurance policy
    2. Because harnesses often break when someone falls
    3. Someone hanging in a harness can quickly become ill
    4. So the worker can climb back up on their own
    Show the answer

    Answer C: Someone hanging in a harness can quickly become ill

    A person hanging in a harness can lose consciousness and suffer serious harm within minutes, so they must be rescued quickly by a planned method.

    Source: Work at Height Regulations 2005 reg 4

  6. Question 6 · Choose one answer

    You are working in a MEWP basket at height when the wind gets much stronger and the basket starts to sway.

    What should you do?

    1. Lower the platform and stop work
    2. Unclip your lanyard so you can move freely
    3. Raise the platform above the gusts
    4. Finish quickly while holding the rails
    Show the answer

    Answer A: Lower the platform and stop work

    Every MEWP has a maximum wind speed. Strong gusts can overturn the machine or throw you against nearby structures. Lower the platform, stop and only carry on when the wind is within the maker's limit.

    Source: IPAF and HSE guidance on MEWPs

Revision notes

What counts as work at height

Work at height means work in any place where you could fall a distance that could injure you, including falling from ground level into an opening or excavation. Falls from height are the biggest single cause of deaths in construction. The law sets an order to follow: avoid working at height where you can; if you cannot, prevent falls with a safe place or collective protection such as guardrails; and only then reduce the distance and effect of a fall with nets, airbags or a harness.

Ladders

Ladders are for short, light, low-risk jobs, as a rough guide no more than about 30 minutes in one position. Set the ladder at 75 degrees, one unit out for every four up. For access, it should rise at least 1 metre above the landing unless there is another handhold. Secure it, keep three points of contact, do not overreach and check stiles, rungs and feet before use. A damaged ladder must be taken out of use.

Stepladders

Open a stepladder fully and make sure any locking devices are engaged. Face the steps, keep your body within the stiles and do not work side-on to the job. Do not stand on the top three steps, including the platform, unless there is a suitable handhold. Move the stepladder rather than leaning out to reach, and never use it on uneven or soft ground or rest it on objects to gain height.

Mobile towers

Only people trained to do it should put up, alter or take down a mobile access tower, following the maker's instructions. Before use, check the wheels are braked, outriggers or stabilisers are fitted, and the platform has guardrails and toe boards. Climb up the inside using the built-in ladder or stairs. Never move a tower with anyone or anything on it, and do not use it on soft or sloping ground or in high winds.

Scaffolds

Only competent scaffolders may erect, alter or dismantle a scaffold. Never remove ties, boards, guardrails or braces yourself, even for a moment. A scaffold must be inspected before first use, at least every 7 days, and after anything that may affect it, such as high winds or an alteration. Most sites use a tag at the access point to show whether the scaffold is safe to use. No tag, or a "do not use" tag, means keep off.

Edge protection and openings

Open edges need protection that stops people and materials falling. Guardrails should have a main rail at least 950 mm high, with an intermediate rail so that no gap is more than 470 mm, and toe boards at the bottom. Holes in floors and roofs must be covered with a cover that is strong enough, fixed so it cannot move and clearly marked, or guarded with rails. Never remove a cover or guardrail without permission and a replacement control.

Fragile surfaces

Fibre cement sheets, roof lights, old glass and some ageing metal sheets cannot take a person's weight, even if they look solid. Never walk on or lean on them. Work on or near fragile roofs needs careful planning: staging or crawling boards, covers or nets under the work area, edge protection and warning signs. Stick to the safe route you have been given and never take a shortcut across the roof.

MEWPs and harnesses

Mobile elevating work platforms (MEWPs) must only be operated by trained, authorised people. In a boom-type MEWP, wear a harness with a short restraint lanyard clipped to the anchor point in the basket, so you cannot be thrown out. Never climb out of the basket or stand on its rails. Watch for overhead structures that could trap you against the controls. Whenever a harness is used, there must be a plan to rescue someone who falls.

Falling objects

Objects falling from height can kill people below, even those wearing helmets. Stop things falling with toe boards, brick guards and netting, keep platforms tidy, and do not stack materials above the toe board. Use tool lanyards where they are needed. Never throw materials or waste down: use a rubbish chute or lower them in a controlled way. Exclusion zones should keep people out of areas below work at height.
