Excavations and underground services: free HS&E test practice questions

Preventing collapse, edge protection, inspections and finding buried services before digging.

6 original questions from the HiVis app for the CITB HS&E Operatives test, each with the answer and an explanation. No sign-up, nothing saved. These are not the real test questions: no app or website can show you those.

  1. Question 1 · Choose one answer

    At what depth can an excavation collapse and seriously injure someone working in it?

    1. Only when it is deeper than 2 metres
    2. Only when it is deeper than 1.2 metres
    3. At any depth, even a shallow one
    4. Only if the ground is sandy or loose
    Show the answer

    Answer C: At any depth, even a shallow one

    There is no safe depth. Even a shallow trench can collapse onto someone kneeling or bending in it, and soil is very heavy.

    Source: HSE guidance on excavation safety

  2. Question 2 · Choose one answer

    A workmate is in a trench about 1.8 metres deep with vertical clay sides and no support. He asks you to jump in and help him connect a pipe quickly.

    What should you do?

    1. Jump in, as clay sides are strong enough
    2. Go in but stay close to the ladder
    3. Stay out, get him out and tell your supervisor
    4. Hold the sides back with plywood while you work
    Show the answer

    Answer C: Stay out, get him out and tell your supervisor

    Vertical, unsupported sides can collapse without warning. Nobody should be in the trench until it is supported or battered back. Get him out and report it.

    Source: CDM 2015 reg 22

  3. Question 3 · Select TWO answers

    Which TWO protect people from falling into an open excavation? Select TWO.

    1. Barriers or guardrails round the edge
    2. A line of traffic cones around it
    3. Strong covers fixed over small holes
    4. Warning tape tied between the spoil heaps
    Show the answer

    Answer A: Barriers or guardrails round the edge, C: Strong covers fixed over small holes

    Guardrails and secure covers physically stop falls. Cones and tape only warn people and will not stop anyone falling in.

    Source: CDM 2015 reg 22(2)

  4. Question 4 · Choose one answer

    You are digging close to a buried service that has been located and marked. How should you dig?

    1. With a pick, working quickly to expose it
    2. With the excavator, but with the bucket teeth raised
    3. Carefully by hand, with a spade or shovel
    4. With a fork driven in hard at an angle
    Show the answer

    Answer C: Carefully by hand, with a spade or shovel

    Near located services, dig by hand with care. Picks, forks and machines can easily pierce cables and pipes.

    Source: HSE HSG47

  5. Question 5 · Choose one answer

    Why should a petrol-driven water pump not be run in the bottom of a trench?

    1. It will flood the trench faster than it empties it
    2. It is too noisy for the people working in the trench
    3. Its exhaust fumes can build up in the trench
    4. It will slowly sink down into the mud
    Show the answer

    Answer C: Its exhaust fumes can build up in the trench

    Exhaust gases, including carbon monoxide, can collect in a trench and poison people working in it. Keep engines out of excavations.

    Source: HSE guidance on excavations and confined spaces

  6. Question 6 · Choose one answer

    Why are trial holes dug by hand near located services before machine digging?

    1. To confirm exactly where the services are
    2. To test whether the soil is good for building
    3. To give the excavator a place to start
    4. To collect soil samples for the lab
    Show the answer

    Answer A: To confirm exactly where the services are

    Plans and detectors only give an approximate position and depth. Careful hand digging exposes the service so its exact line is known before a machine works nearby.

    Source: HSE HSG47

Revision notes

Why excavations collapse

The sides of an excavation can collapse suddenly and without warning. A single cubic metre of soil weighs more than a tonne, so even a small fall can crush or bury a person. There is no "safe" depth: people have been killed in trenches that were not much deeper than knee height while working bent over. Rain, frost, drying out, nearby traffic and vibration can all weaken the sides.

Supporting the sides

Excavations are made safe by supporting the sides, with trench boxes, sheet piles or hydraulic props, or by battering them back to a safe slope or stepping them. Support must go in without anyone working in an unsupported section. Never enter an excavation with unsupported vertical sides that could collapse on you, and never remove or adjust support yourself unless that is your trained job.

Edges and access

Protect people from falling in with barriers or guardrails, or secure covers over small holes. Keep spoil heaps, materials and plant back from the edge, because the extra weight can make the sides give way. Use fixed stop blocks to stop vehicles tipping into the excavation. Provide safe access, usually a ladder that is tied and rises above the edge, and never climb the supports.

Inspections

A competent person must inspect excavations where people work at the start of each shift, after anything likely to affect their strength or stability, such as heavy rain or a vehicle striking the support, and after any accidental fall of rock, earth or other material. Look out yourself for cracks along the edge, bulging sides, water coming in and loose support, and get out and report them straight away.

Underground services

Buried electricity cables, gas pipes and water mains can kill or injure when struck. Before digging, use service plans, then a trained person locates and marks services with a cable avoidance tool (CAT) and signal generator (Genny). Plans and detectors are not always accurate, so dig trial holes by hand near services, using a spade or shovel rather than a pick or fork. Treat every cable as live. If you hit or uncover an unknown service, stop and report it.

Gas and fumes

If you smell gas or damage a gas pipe, stop work at once, get everyone away, keep ignition sources such as engines, cigarettes and phones away, and call the National Gas Emergency number, 0800 111 999. Excavations can also fill with harmful gases and fumes, including exhaust from petrol or diesel plant, which can collect at the bottom. Some excavations are confined spaces and need extra controls.
