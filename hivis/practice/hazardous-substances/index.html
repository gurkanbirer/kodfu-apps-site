Hazardous substances and dust: free HS&E test practice questions

COSHH, hazard symbols, safety data sheets, silica and wood dust, and how exposure is controlled.

6 original questions from the HiVis app for the CITB HS&E Operatives test, each with the answer and an explanation. No sign-up, nothing saved. These are not the real test questions: no app or website can show you those.

  1. Question 1 · Choose one answer

    What do the letters COSHH stand for?

    1. Construction Of Safe Homes and Housing
    2. Control Of Substances Hazardous to Health
    3. Care Of Site Health and Hygiene
    4. Control Of Site Hazards and Heights
    Show the answer

    Answer B: Control Of Substances Hazardous to Health

    COSHH is the Control of Substances Hazardous to Health Regulations 2002. It makes employers assess and control exposure to harmful substances, from cement and solvents to dusts and germs, to protect workers' health.

    Source: Control of Substances Hazardous to Health Regulations 2002

  2. Question 2 · Choose one answer

    A hazard symbol shows the outline of a person with a star-shaped burst on the chest. What does it warn of?

    1. The product may explode if it is heated or struck
    2. The product is a mild skin irritant only
    3. Serious long-term health effects, such as cancer
    4. The product is a gas stored under pressure
    Show the answer

    Answer C: Serious long-term health effects, such as cancer

    This "health hazard" symbol warns of serious long-term effects, such as cancer, damage to organs or allergy affecting breathing.

    Source: GB CLP

  3. Question 3 · Choose one answer

    You are asked to cut paving slabs with a petrol saw. The water bottle on the saw is empty and the supervisor wants the job finished quickly.

    What should you do?

    1. Cut them dry and stand upwind of the dust
    2. Cut them dry while wearing a paper dust mask
    3. Refill the water and get the suppression working
    4. Cut them dry, then damp the area down afterwards
    Show the answer

    Answer C: Refill the water and get the suppression working

    Dry cutting creates large amounts of silica dust. Get the water suppression working first, and wear the RPE the assessment requires.

    Source: HSE CIS36 and COSHH 2002 reg 7

  4. Question 4 · Choose one answer

    You have a few days' beard growth. You have been told to wear a tight-fitting FFP3 mask for drilling concrete.

    What should you do?

    1. Wear it anyway and pull the straps tighter
    2. Wear it and hold it tightly to your face while drilling
    3. Tell your supervisor and shave or get a powered hood
    4. Tie a scarf over the mask for extra protection
    Show the answer

    Answer C: Tell your supervisor and shave or get a powered hood

    Stubble stops a tight-fitting mask sealing, so dust leaks in. You need to be clean-shaven or use a type that does not rely on a face seal.

    Source: HSE HSG53 and face-fit testing guidance

  5. Question 5 · Choose one answer

    When must a face-fit test be done for a tight-fitting mask?

    1. For each model and size of mask you will wear
    2. Only once in your working life, for any mask
    3. Only if you have a beard or stubble
    4. Only when the mask is worn for over four hours
    Show the answer

    Answer A: For each model and size of mask you will wear

    Faces differ, so a mask that seals on one person can leak on another. Each make, model and size of tight-fitting mask you use must be fit tested on you before you rely on it, with you clean-shaven.

    Source: HSE: Fit testing basics (RPE)

  6. Question 6 · Choose one answer

    What is a workplace exposure limit (WEL)?

    1. The most you may breathe in over a set time
    2. The safe distance to keep from a dusty task
    3. The longest time you may wear a dust mask
    4. The amount of a product you may store
    Show the answer

    Answer A: The most you may breathe in over a set time

    WELs, published by HSE in EH40, cap the average amount of a substance in the air you breathe over a set time, usually 8 hours. For respirable silica the WEL is 0.1 mg/m³, a tiny amount, so dust must be controlled at source.

    Source: HSE EH40 Workplace exposure limits

Revision notes

COSHH

The Control of Substances Hazardous to Health Regulations 2002 (COSHH) require employers to assess the risks from hazardous substances and then prevent or adequately control exposure. Avoiding or substituting a substance comes first, then controls such as extraction and ventilation, with PPE last. Controls must be maintained and used. On site, hazardous substances include cement, solvents, paints, resins, fuels, dusts, fumes and germs in sewage or soil.

Labels and safety data sheets

Hazardous products carry hazard pictograms: a black symbol on a white background inside a red diamond. Examples include a skull and crossbones (toxic in small amounts), an exclamation mark (harmful or irritant), corrosion (burns skin and metal), a flame (flammable) and a person with a star on the chest (serious longer-term health effects such as cancer). The supplier's safety data sheet gives hazards, safe handling, PPE and first aid. Keep products in their original, labelled containers.

Silica dust

Cutting, drilling, grinding or chasing concrete, brick, block, stone, mortar and tiles creates respirable crystalline silica, a dust fine enough to reach deep into the lungs. It causes silicosis, lung cancer and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), which can kill. The dangerous particles are too fine to see. Control it at source with water suppression or on-tool extraction, and wear suitable RPE as well where the assessment says so.

Wood and other dusts

Wood dust, including MDF, can cause asthma, and hardwood dust can cause nasal cancer. Use on-tool extraction and extraction units, not dry sweeping or compressed air, which put fine dust back into the air you breathe. Clean up with a suitable vacuum cleaner (usually M or H class for construction dust) or wet methods. Dust from all sources builds up over a working life, so control it every time.

Respiratory protective equipment

Respiratory protective equipment (RPE) is used when exposure cannot be controlled enough by other means. It must suit the hazard: dust masks do not stop gases or vapours. FFP3 masks are commonly used for silica. Tight-fitting masks must be face-fit tested for you, and you must be clean-shaven where the mask seals, as stubble lets dust in. If you cannot be clean-shaven, a powered hood is an option. Replace filters as instructed.

Solvents, fumes and lead

Solvents in paints, adhesives and cleaners can cause headaches, dizziness and sickness, and long-term harm to the nervous system and organs. Use them with good ventilation and replace lids. Welding and hot cutting fumes can cause lung cancer, so they need extraction. Old paint and roofing may contain lead: avoid creating dust or fumes, and wash before eating or smoking, because lead is mostly taken in by swallowing.

Cement and asthma

Wet cement is strongly alkaline. It can cause serious burns, especially if it gets inside boots or gloves or you kneel in it, as well as dermatitis. Occupational asthma can be caused by substances such as isocyanates in some paints and foams, and by wood and flour dust. Report symptoms early, such as wheezing or breathlessness that is worse at work and better on days off.

Exposure limits and extraction checks

Many hazardous substances have a workplace exposure limit (WEL), the most that may be in the air you breathe, averaged over a set time. The WEL for respirable crystalline silica is 0.1 mg/m³, a tiny amount, which is why control at source matters. Local exhaust ventilation, including on-tool extraction, must be thoroughly examined and tested at least every 14 months, and users check it works before each use, for example with the airflow indicator. Disposable masks are rated FFP1, FFP2 or FFP3, and FFP3 gives the highest protection.
