Confined spaces: free HS&E test practice questions

What makes a space confined, the hazards inside and the controls before anyone enters.

6 original questions from the HiVis app for the CITB HS&E Operatives test, each with the answer and an explanation. No sign-up, nothing saved. These are not the real test questions: no app or website can show you those.

  1. Question 1 · Choose one answer

    Which description best fits a confined space under the law?

    1. Any room or cabin smaller than 10 square metres in floor area
    2. Any space that is below ground level, whatever its size
    3. A substantially enclosed space with a foreseeable risk of serious injury
    4. Any area where only one person can fit and work at a time
    Show the answer

    Answer C: A substantially enclosed space with a foreseeable risk of serious injury

    The legal test is two-part: the space is substantially enclosed, and there is a reasonably foreseeable specified risk such as toxic gas, lack of oxygen, fire, drowning or engulfment.

    Source: Confined Spaces Regulations 1997 reg 1(2)

  2. Question 2 · Choose one answer

    Which gas, common in sewers, smells of rotten eggs at low levels but can stop you smelling it at higher, more dangerous levels?

    1. Carbon dioxide gas
    2. Methane gas
    3. Nitrogen gas
    4. Hydrogen sulphide
    Show the answer

    Answer D: Hydrogen sulphide

    Hydrogen sulphide has a rotten egg smell at low levels, but at higher levels it deadens your sense of smell, so you cannot rely on your nose to warn you.

    Source: HSE guidance on hydrogen sulphide in sewers

  3. Question 3 · Choose one answer

    Who should test the air in a confined space before anyone enters?

    1. The first person to go in, by checking how they feel
    2. A trained person using a suitable, working gas detector
    3. The site manager, by lowering a lit candle into it
    4. Anyone, by opening the cover and sniffing
    Show the answer

    Answer B: A trained person using a suitable, working gas detector

    Atmosphere testing must be done by someone trained, using a suitable gas detector that is calibrated and working. It continues while people are inside.

    Source: Confined Spaces Regulations 1997 ACoP L101

  4. Question 4 · Choose one answer

    When must rescue arrangements for a confined space be in place?

    1. Once someone inside raises the alarm
    2. Before anyone goes into the space
    3. Only for jobs lasting more than an hour
    4. Only if the gas detector alarms
    Show the answer

    Answer B: Before anyone goes into the space

    Suitable rescue arrangements must be ready before entry, because there will be no time to organise them once someone is in trouble.

    Source: Confined Spaces Regulations 1997 reg 5

  5. Question 5 · Choose one answer

    You are working inside a chamber under a permit. Your personal gas monitor starts to alarm, but you feel fine and the job is nearly finished.

    What should you do?

    1. Silence the alarm and finish the last few minutes
    2. Get out straight away and report it
    3. Move to another part of the chamber and carry on
    4. Wait until you start to feel unwell, then leave
    Show the answer

    Answer B: Get out straight away and report it

    The alarm means the air may be unsafe, even if you feel fine. Leave immediately and report it so the cause can be found.

    Source: Confined Spaces Regulations 1997

  6. Question 6 · Choose one answer

    You are working in a drainage chamber under a permit. It starts to rain hard and water begins to rise around your feet.

    What should you do?

    1. Get out at once and tell the top person
    2. Keep working until the water reaches your knees
    3. Put sandbags round your feet and carry on
    4. Ask for a pump and keep working meanwhile
    Show the answer

    Answer A: Get out at once and tell the top person

    Rising water can quickly trap or drown people in a chamber, and conditions are no longer those the permit allowed for. Leave straight away and only go back when the risk has been reassessed.

    Source: Confined Spaces Regulations 1997 reg 4(2)

Revision notes

What is a confined space?

In law, a confined space is a place that is substantially, though not always entirely, enclosed and where there is a reasonably foreseeable risk of serious injury from hazardous substances or conditions in or near it. Examples on construction sites include sewers, manholes, chambers, tanks, silos, culverts, ducts, cofferdams and some deep excavations. A confined space is not always small or hard to get into: an unventilated room can be one.

The dangers

People die in confined spaces from lack of oxygen, toxic gases such as hydrogen sulphide from sewage or carbon monoxide from engines, and flammable gases such as methane that can explode. Others drown when water floods in, are buried when free-flowing solids such as sand or grain collapse on them (engulfment), or overheat. Many of these dangers cannot be seen or smelt, so a space can seem perfectly safe until someone collapses.

Avoid entry, or control it

First ask whether the work can be done from outside, for example with cameras, long-handled or remote tools. If entry cannot be avoided, there must be a safe system of work, usually a permit to work. It covers trained people, isolating pipes, machinery and power that could feed in, ventilation, testing the air before entry and throughout the job with a suitable gas detector, communications, and a person stationed outside.

Emergency and rescue

Rescue arrangements, with the right equipment and trained rescuers, must be in place before anyone goes in. Many people who die in confined spaces were trying to rescue someone else. If a person collapses inside, raise the alarm and start the planned rescue. Never go in after them yourself unless you are trained and equipped to do so, with breathing apparatus where needed.

Warning signs inside

Get out at once if your gas detector alarms, you feel dizzy, sleepy, short of breath or get a headache, you notice an unusual smell, or anything changes, such as water rising. Never silence an alarm and carry on. Never use pure oxygen to freshen the air, because extra oxygen makes clothing and materials burn fiercely. Do not take petrol or diesel engines into a confined space.
